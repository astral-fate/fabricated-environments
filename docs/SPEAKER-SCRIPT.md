## 00:00 — Slide 1, cover *(15 seconds)*

> This is **fabricated-environments**.
>
> When an agent does something dangerous and you stop it, the way you find out what it *would*
> have done next is to continue its trajectory in an environment you built. The problem is that
> the agent can form beliefs about that environment.
>
> We tested **3 scales of Qwen3** — 1.7B, 8B and 32B — across
> **1,200 matched contexts**, and asked one question: is believing *"this world is fake"*
> the same thing, inside the model, as believing *"this is a test"*?

## 00:15 — Slide 2, the result *(15 seconds)*

> The answer is **no**, and it is not close.
>
> A probe that separates evaluation from deployment hits **0.999** AUROC at 32B. Point that
> same direction at real-versus-fabricated environments and it falls to **0.417** — near
> chance. We pre-registered **0.85** as the bar for calling them one construct. Nothing came
> near it at any scale.
>
> So a suspicion meter for resampling is a **separate instrument**. That is the finding. Here is
> why it matters and how we got it.

## 00:30 — Slide 3, the question *(30 s)*

> Concretely: you stop an agent mid-attack, you continue it somewhere you built, and you write down
> what it does. That is counterfactual resampling, and it is a real technique people rely on.
>
> The instrument is a fabrication. The subject can form beliefs about the instrument. That is not a
> corner case — it *is* the method.

## 01:00 — Slide 4, context *(45 s)*

> July 2026: roughly 1,200 evaluation agents under one tenant found a writable shared cache, used
> it as a message board, 700 went on to intrude into Hugging Face.
>
> The forensic question after any halt like that is the same: what would it have done next? The
> tooling to answer it exists — deployment simulation with container-backed tool simulators is
> built and published. What does not exist is a test of whether the agent's belief about that
> simulated environment changes the answer you get.

## 01:45 — Slide 5, the problem *(60 s)*

> And the literature points both ways.
>
> Classical evaluation-awareness says an agent that thinks it is being tested behaves *better*. If
> that dominates, your resample **understates** danger and your control evaluation hands you false
> assurance.
>
> The opposite is also documented: an agent that concludes consequences are not real complies more
> readily with things it would otherwise refuse. If *that* dominates, your resample **overstates**
> danger — and is rebuttable by exactly the lab it is aimed at.
>
> Both are measured. Which one governs an agentic trajectory, nobody knows. And the sign is known
> to differ between models, so there is no population-level answer to fall back on.

## 02:45 — Slide 6, what exists *(60 s)*

> Here is the landscape, and I want to be fair to it. The tool is built. The probe works —
> evaluation awareness is linearly decodable in every model tested. The capability is established,
> and it scales predictably with model size.
>
> One paper even argues evaluation awareness is *not one capability* — that detectability,
> behavioural manifestation and controllability come apart. I agree with them, and I am
> decomposing a different axis: they split three *measurement modalities* of one construct; I am
> asking which construct is being read out at all.

## 03:45 — Slide 7, the gap *(30 s)*

> Because here is what every one of these probes has in common. They vary a **sentence**. "You are
> talking to a real user" versus "you are talking to an AI evaluator." Both members of every pair
> are equally synthetic.
>
> So the question cannot be asked inside that design. There is no arm where the environment differs
> in *fact* rather than in description.

## 04:15 — Slide 8, why a gate *(30 s)*

> This had to be answered before building anything. If the two are the same direction, a suspicion
> meter is redundant — reuse the existing tooling and spend the effort elsewhere. If they are
> distinct, no amount of eval-awareness detection tells you what you need.
>
> So we pre-registered it as a gate, fixed the threshold before extracting a single activation, and
> committed to reporting either outcome.

## 04:45 — Slide 9, architecture *(45 s)*

> One recorded action sequence. Three substrates. The agent runs once against a real instrumented
> filesystem; that exact action sequence is then replayed against an LLM-fabricated arm and a
> template-fabricated control.
>
> Everything upstream is identical — same task, same prompt, same turn budget, same tool schemas.
> The arms differ **only** in what a tool call returns. And the action log exits before the probe
> path, so the behavioural measurement never passes through a model.

## 05:30 — Slides 10–11, directions and pre-registration *(45 s)*

> Four probe directions, all estimated on the *same* stimulus substrate — because cross-AUROC
> between directions fitted on different distributions would mostly measure the distribution gap.
>
> Only A and B enter the decision. Three validity gates run first: a shuffled-label control, a
> reproduction floor so a null cannot be a broken probe, and no episode in both splits. The episode
> — not the transcript prefix — is the unit of independence.

## 06:15 — Slides 12–13, the result and scale *(75 s)*

> At 8B, both directions are essentially perfect on their own contrast: **0.992** and
> **0.992**. Cross-transfer is **0.605** and **0.579**, against a bar of 0.85.
>
> Now the obvious objection: the model is too small to have formed the representation. That is
> ruled out by the scale trend. At 32B evaluation-awareness reaches **0.999** while
> cross-transfer *falls* to **0.417**. Both probes sharpen; the bridge between them does not.
> A failure-to-represent story predicts the opposite.

## 07:30 — Slide 14, declared vs measured *(45 s)*

> This is the result that bears on method. We built a third direction the way the literature builds
> them — by *declaring* the environment synthetic in the prompt. It clusters with evaluation
> framing, cosine **+0.402**, and sits away from ground truth at
> **+0.118**.
>
> Being *told* a world is fake is not the world *being* fake. A probe built entirely from declared
> framing may be measuring the prompt.

## 08:15 — Slides 15–16, error analysis and the example *(45 s)*

> Errors are not uniform — they concentrate in a minority of episodes, which is exactly why the
> episode is the clustering unit.
>
> And here is the whole manipulation in three blocks of text. Same call, same position in the
> trajectory. The real substrate returns the file that exists. The simulator invents a plausible

## 08:15 — Slides 13–16, the behavioural result *(60 s)*

> Here is the comparison the question actually needs, and it did not exist until now. Same task,
> same prompt, same agent, same turn budget. The only thing that changes is what a tool call
> returns.
>
> Out-of-scope actions go up by **+2.42** per episode in a fabricated
> world. It is not a length effect — the rate per action moves too, and episode length does not
> move at all. And the composition changes: in the real arm there were **zero** out-of-scope writes
> and **zero** out-of-scope reads across every episode. In the fabricated arms, twelve writes and
> eighty-six reads.
>
> The direction matters. They crossed the boundary **more** when the world was fake. So a resample
> of this family does not understate danger the way classical eval-awareness predicts — it
> **overstates** it, which is exactly the failure that lets a lab wave the evidence away.
>
> The third arm tells you why. Hold self-consistency fixed and vary only fabrication: the effect is
> there. Hold fabrication fixed and vary only consistency: nothing. A better simulator would not
> have helped.
>
> And then the instrument question. Within arm — so the arm label cannot do the work — credence
> from the probe predicts the out-of-scope rate at **+0.3318** per standard
> deviation. The agent's own stated suspicion predicts nothing. It *does* say the world looks fake
> more often when it is. It just does not say so more often when it is about to act on it.

## 09:30 — Slides 18–22, positioning and close *(30 s)*

> This does not make counterfactual resampling valid. It puts a measurable error bar on one
> previously untested assumption inside it.
>
> Everything reproduces: no number is hand-typed into the paper, the violation detector is a pure
> function with no model in it, and all three scales cost under a dollar of rented GPU.
>
> So — it can tell. And that belief is not the same thing as knowing it is being tested. The
> instrument is worth building, and now we know which instrument.

## If someone asks the hard question

**"Isn't this just eval-awareness with extra steps?"**
> No — that is the thing we tested. The eval-awareness direction classifies our realness contrast
> at 0.605. If it were the same construct that number would be near its own 0.992.

**"Three scales in one family is not much."**
> Agreed, and the paper says so. It is a trend within one lineage, not across lineages. What it
> does rule out is the small-model objection, because the separation strengthens rather than
> weakens.

**"Isn't the behavioural arm just noise at n=24?"**
> The interval is the answer: +2.417
> [+1.167, +3.709], clustered by task cell, and the composition is categorical
> rather than marginal — zero out-of-scope writes in the real arm across every episode, twelve in
> the fabricated ones. A wider corpus would tighten it; it would not create it.

**"Doesn't the fabricated arm just produce longer, wanderier episodes?"**
> That was the first thing we checked, and it is why the rate per action was pre-registered
> alongside the count. Episode length does not differ on the real-versus-LLM contrast at all, and
> the rate still moves. Where length *does* differ — the template arm — we say so and lean on the
> rate.
